A property condition assessment (PCA) in Greenville-Spartanburg, SC is a walk-through survey and document review of a commercial property that identifies physical deficiencies, immediate repairs and expected capital needs. The Upstate's commercial stock runs from early 20th-century textile mills converted to apartments and offices to modern bulk distribution buildings along I-85 and I-26, and each type carries a different capital profile.

## Why this matters for Greenville-Spartanburg buyers and lenders

Greenville-Spartanburg is one of the largest industrial markets in the Southeast, and new supply keeps arriving. That makes physical condition a pricing issue: older buildings compete directly with new construction, and the buyer who underestimates a roof or slab replacement overpays.

[CBRE's Q3 2026 Greenville-Spartanburg industrial figures](https://www.cbre.com/insights/figures/greenville-spartanburg-industrial-figures-q3-2026) reported vacancy of 5.0%, about 685,000 square feet of positive net absorption in the quarter, and a construction pipeline that grew from 2.09 million to 3.32 million square feet. [Lee & Associates' Q3 2026 report](https://www.lee-associates.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/2026-Q3-Greenville-Spartanburg-SC-Industrial.pdf), which uses a different methodology, put vacancy at 7.5% across an inventory of roughly 258 million square feet, with about 3.4 million square feet under construction. Either way, owners of second-generation buildings need a clear view of capital needs to compete for tenants and to support refinancing.

The region's other defining asset type is the converted textile mill. Judson Mill in Greenville, for example, began operating in 1912, grew to a campus of about 1,000,000 square feet, ran until 2015 and is now being redeveloped as a mixed-use community with loft apartments, retail and event space, according to [Greenville's Metro Connects](https://metroconnects.org/the-textile-crescent-judson-mill/). Mill conversions like this one combine a century-old shell with new systems, and both timelines belong in the capital plan.

## What does a commercial PCA cover in Greenville-Spartanburg?

A commercial PCA covers the major building systems and the site, and turns what the assessor observes into a prioritized list of repairs and a forecast of capital needs. Most PCAs for lenders and institutional buyers are written to [ASTM E2018](https://store.astm.org/e2018-24.html), the industry's baseline guide for property condition assessments.

A typical scope includes:

- **Roofing:** covering, flashing, parapets, drainage and the attachment of rooftop equipment
- **Structure and envelope:** foundations, framing, masonry, exterior walls, sealants, windows, doors and balconies
- **Mechanical, electrical and plumbing:** HVAC equipment age and condition, electrical service and distribution, domestic water and sanitary systems
- **Life safety and fire safety:** fire alarm and sprinkler observations, egress, emergency lighting and fire extinguisher service tags
- **Accessibility:** a visual survey of common accessibility features
- **Site:** paving, parking, curbs, sidewalks, stormwater drainage, retaining walls and site lighting
- **Documents and interviews:** available drawings, permits, service records, prior reports and conversations with property management

The deliverable is a report with photographs, an immediate repair list and a capital reserve table for the expected hold or loan term. Agree the standard, the exclusions and the deliverables in writing before fieldwork starts.

## What should a PCA look for in I-85 and I-26 industrial buildings?

In Upstate industrial and flex buildings, the capital budget usually turns on the roof, the slab, the dock equipment, the truck court and the fire protection system.

- **Roofs:** Large low-slope roofs on 1990s and 2000s warehouses are often at or past their first replacement cycle. Record roof type, age, ponding, flashing condition, past repairs and warranty status.
- **Slabs:** Note cracking, joint spalling, settlement and curling, and any sign of heavy point loads from earlier manufacturing or racking.
- **Dock equipment:** Count and assess dock doors, levelers, seals, bumpers and restraints, which wear quickly under the region's heavy truck traffic.
- **Truck courts and paving:** Asphalt and concrete fail first at drainage points, turning areas and trailer storage. Note rutting, alligator cracking and ponding.
- **Fire protection:** Record sprinkler system type, visible condition, fire pump presence and service tags. A change from manufacturing to high-piled storage, or the reverse, can raise sprinkler design questions that a fire protection engineer should confirm.
- **Building systems for manufacturing tenants:** Plants serving the region's automotive and advanced manufacturing supply chain often have heavy electrical service, compressed air, cranes or process piping. A PCA records base building systems and their condition, but does not evaluate process equipment.

## What should a PCA look for in converted textile mills?

Converted mills need closer attention to masonry, structure, roofs and the age of the systems added during the conversion. The shell may be more than 100 years old while the HVAC and elevators are 10 to 20 years old.

### Masonry and water management

Multi-story brick mills depend on sound mortar, lintels, parapets and roof drainage to keep water out. Look for eroded mortar joints, cracked or spalled brick, efflorescence, rusting steel lintels, displaced parapet coping and failed sealant around replacement windows. Repointing and parapet work can be large line items, and deferring them lets water reach finishes and structure.

### Structure and floors

Many mills were built with heavy timber or early concrete frames sized for looms and spinning machinery. The assessor should note visible cracking or spalling in concrete, checking, rot or water staining in timber, and members that were cut or notched for later mechanical work. Concealed conditions may need a structural engineer to follow up.

### Conversion-era systems

HVAC, electrical distribution, elevators, plumbing and fire protection installed during conversion should be dated from nameplates, permits and service records. Plan for staggered replacement rather than treating a renovated building as new.

### Tax credits and historic status

Several Upstate mill conversions have used state textile mill credits or historic tax credits, which can restrict how exterior repairs are done. A PCA does not determine credit eligibility or compliance. Confirm those questions with counsel and review the [South Carolina Textiles Communities Revitalization Act](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t12c065.php) before you budget exterior work.

## What should a PCA look for in Upstate multifamily properties?

For apartment communities, the capital budget usually turns on exterior wood components, roofs, unit HVAC and site drainage.

- **Stairs, balconies and breezeways:** Wood stairs and balconies on 1970s through 1990s garden communities deteriorate under the Upstate's humid summers. Look for rot, loose railings, corroded connectors and failed flashing at ledger boards.
- **Siding and trim:** Note damaged siding, trim and sealant, and evidence of water behind the cladding.
- **Roofs:** Record roof age, covering type and evidence of leaks, wind damage or patching on each building.
- **Unit HVAC and water heaters:** Sample units and record equipment ages, since staggered replacement is often the largest recurring reserve item.
- **Site drainage and slopes:** The Piedmont's clay soils drain slowly, and many Upstate sites are graded on slopes. Look for erosion, retaining wall movement, ponding, settled walks and pavement failure near downspouts and inlets.

## How do lenders use a PCA on Greenville-Spartanburg deals?

Lenders use the PCA to size required repairs and capital reserves and to confirm that the collateral is in the condition the borrower describes. Many commercial lenders, along with agency, CMBS and SBA programs, set their own report requirements on top of ASTM E2018, such as a specific reserve term, cost tables or a particular report format.

## Who handles building codes and permits in the Upstate?

Code and permit questions go to the jurisdiction where the property sits, which may be a city or the county. Unincorporated Greenville County properties fall under [Greenville County Building Safety](https://greenvillecountysc.gov/BuildingSafety/Default.aspx), while properties inside the City of Greenville, the City of Spartanburg or another municipality are handled by that city's building and planning staff.

A PCA can request permit history and open-violation records as part of document review, and it can note visible conditions that look like unpermitted work. It does not certify code compliance. If permit status matters to the deal, ask for a separate zoning and permit report.

## What does a PCA not replace?

A PCA reports physical conditions that can be observed during the site visit. It does not replace a Phase I environmental site assessment, a zoning report, title review, lease review, a survey, a tax credit or historic review, or legal advice. It is not a code compliance inspection. Concealed conditions and systems that were not tested may need follow-up by a structural engineer, roofer, mechanical contractor or other specialist. Former mill and manufacturing sites often warrant a Phase I as well.
